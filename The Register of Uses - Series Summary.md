# The Register of Uses — Series Summary & Notes

*A four-book gaslamp fantasy romance, by Aspen Aye. Single point of view throughout: Mercy Rooke, first person, past tense. Set in an alternate Regency–early-Victorian England where parish registers, rate-books, and the law of settlement are not backdrop but the machinery of fate.*

## The premise

Mercy Rooke dies and comes back — eighteen years old, in her own bed, in the parish she was born to but never chose. She is a keeper of accounts: of clocks, of clocks' weights, and then of the parish register itself. The series asks what it means to be an entry in someone else's book, and what it costs to write your own line. Each book turns on a different kind of "register of uses" — the uses a thing or a person is put to, and who gets to decide.

## The through-line

- **Book One** establishes the world and the magic: *Keeping* — the art of pressing what should end down onto the right side of an ending. The ledger only goes out, never in; money buys comfort, land buys life. Mercy learns she can keep things, and learns the price.
- **Book Two** moves the question to the body and the household: what a woman is permitted to say, and what she withholds. This is the gaslamp fantasy romance proper; the romantic line begins here and runs early, dense, and — by design — not chaste.
- **Book Three** is the quietest and most political: a woman the register does not have, an order that says only *It is entirely lawful*, and the question *what do you want* that Mercy cannot yet answer for herself.
- **Book Four** closes the arc: Mercy, now twenty-one, must ask one question for herself — of the one woman in England who has spent forty years refusing to be asked. *Ask me. I will answer.*

## Continuity anchors

Single source of truth for all dates and ages: `series_chronology.md` (in the parent `premium_long` folder). Highlights:

- Mercy born **4 March 1813**.
- Tace born **c. February 1763**; Esther Callard born **c. 1774**; Edward Rooke born **c. 1787**.
- The Tace / Tindle removal order, dated **12 March 1793**, is defective (signed by one justice and a clerk, not two), names no destination parish, and is therefore appealed by Tindle against itself — execution suspended, then struck out at Michaelmas 1794.
- From Michaelmas 1794 to Michaelmas 1834, Tindle pays **£8 a year** instead of executing the order — entered in the overseers' book as *the turning*. Forty years.
- At Michaelmas 1834 the line is struck out; seven households and one subscription from Collow carry the £8 privately. No book records it.

All ages in every book are derived from these birth years, never set by feel. Tace and Esther move together (same 1793 anchor): in 1834 Tace is 71 and Esther is 60. Mercy cannot be in her thirties while Tace is seventy-one — if those ever disagree, the year is right and the age was guessed.

## Voice & style

- One narrator, one tense, across all four books. Mercy's voice is dry and ledger-precise, gradually warmer; the romance is early-dense, mid-physical, late-open-door, by deliberate instruction (written to please a commercial Amazon readership, not to win a literary prize).
- Prose is de-AI'd: no em dashes in body text (scene breaks use `***`), no contractions in narrative, no translation cadence, no filter verbs. Each manuscript passes `novel_check.py` (H1–H9) and `facts_check.py` with zero blockers, and contains no CJK characters. Cross-chapter duplicate sentences are audited to zero.
